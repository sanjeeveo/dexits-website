Seller Discretionary Earnings Explained: How Buyers Value Your Store

Valuation · July 16, 2026 · 6 min read

Seller discretionary earnings explained in one line: it's the total yearly financial benefit one full-time owner pulls out of a business — net profit plus the owner's own pay and any costs a new owner wouldn't inherit. If you run a Shopify or dropshipping store and you're weighing a sale, this is the number that matters. Buyers don't price your store on revenue, and rarely on accounting profit. They price it on SDE, then multiply it. Get SDE right and you understand your own valuation before any broker or marketplace tells you what it "thinks."

Seller discretionary earnings explained: the exact formula

Start with net profit from your P&L, then add back the discretionary and owner-specific costs. The standard build looks like this:

  • Net profit — what's left after all costs, as your books report it.
  • + Owner's salary / draws — the pay you take for working in the business.
  • + One owner benefit — health insurance, a personal phone, a car run through the business.
  • + One-time costs — a rebrand, a legal bill, a store migration that won't repeat.
  • + Non-essential spend — the $3k course or the tool you bought but never operationalized.

The result is seller's discretionary earnings — the honest picture of what the business generates for its owner. This is why two stores with identical revenue can be worth very different amounts: the one with disciplined, well-documented SDE wins.

Tip: Add-backs have to survive a stranger reading your bank statements. "I sometimes expense dinners" is defensible; re-classifying a core ad spend as "one-time" is not. Aggressive add-backs are the fastest way to lose buyer trust in diligence.

SDE vs profit vs revenue — why the distinction sets your price

Revenue is vanity in an acquisition. A store doing $500k in sales but $20k in SDE is a $20k-earning business with a lot of moving parts, and it will be priced accordingly. Accounting profit undersells you, because it buries the salary you pay yourself and legitimate one-time costs inside "expenses." Seller discretionary earnings sits in between and reflects reality: it's what a hands-on buyer could reasonably expect to earn in year one. How buyers turn SDE into an offer

Once SDE is set, a buyer applies a multiple — typically expressed against annual SDE. The multiple is where the store's quality gets priced: history, traffic diversity, supplier concentration, brand, and how much of the work depends on you personally. A clean, defensible store earns a higher multiple on the same SDE. Here's how the same earnings can produce very different outcomes:

$40,0003.0x (established niche, some repeat)$120,000
$40,0004.0x (branded, owned inventory, email list)$160,000

Same SDE, nearly double the price. That spread is the entire argument for cleaning up your earnings and de-risking the business before you list.

Common SDE mistakes that quietly cost you multiple

Most owners leave money on the table in predictable ways. They fail to add back their own salary, so the business looks less profitable than it is. They mix personal and business spending so nothing is provably discretionary. They run a single ad account with no attribution, so a buyer can't trust the profit line. And they wait until they're exhausted to sell, when SDE is already sliding. Every one of those is fixable months before a sale — and the fix is the same work that makes the business easier to run in the meantime.

Tip: Keep a running "add-back log" throughout the year — date, amount, reason. At sale time you hand a buyer evidence instead of asking them to take your word. Documented add-backs convert; verbal ones get discounted.

Know your SDE before anyone else does

The owners who exit well are the ones who can defend their seller discretionary earnings line by line before a buyer ever asks. That's seller discretionary earnings explained in practice: a number you own and can prove, not one a broker hands you. Run the calculation on your own store, keep the receipts, and you turn a vague "what's it worth?" into a number you can stand behind.
